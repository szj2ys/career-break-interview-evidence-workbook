# Career Break Interview Evidence Workbook

Prepare a clear return-to-work explanation and a behavioral interview example using facts you can stand behind. This workbook helps you separate past experience, recent practice and skills you still need to refresh.

It is a set of ordinary Markdown documents. Download or copy the three files, open them in any text editor, or print them. You do not need an account, internet connection, special software, microphone or AI service to complete the workbook.

## Who it is for

Use this if you are returning after a career break and thinking, “My experience is real, but how do I explain what is still relevant?” The break may involve caring responsibilities, study, health, travel or something you prefer not to discuss. You decide how much personal context belongs in your answer.

The workbook is not a hiring assessment. It does not score employability, verify credentials, predict an offer or tell you what an employer will ask.

## Start here

1. Save a private copy of [worksheet.md](worksheet.md). Do not put personal information, references or confidential work material into a public repository, issue or pull request.
2. Choose one real target role and one requirement you want to demonstrate. Use the job description if you have one; otherwise label your role assumptions.
3. Complete the evidence inventory before writing an answer. Distinguish what you did before the break from what you have tried recently.
4. Draft two separate answer cards: a brief return-to-work explanation and a concrete example of a relevant skill. They serve different questions.
5. Check every claim against your evidence. A plan to learn a tool is a plan, not proof that you can already use it.
6. Read the cards aloud or share them privately with a practice partner. Revise any point the listener could misunderstand.

Read [worked-example.md](worked-example.md) if you are stuck. Every person, event and artifact described there is fictional. Its purpose is to show the reasoning, not provide an answer to copy as your own history.

## What you will finish with

- An evidence inventory with dates, your own contribution and the limits of each example.
- A privacy boundary for explaining the break without losing the thread of the interview question.
- A return-to-work answer that separates motivation, recent preparation and unresolved arrangements.
- A behavioral answer that names the problem, your action and the result without borrowing someone else's contribution.
- One concrete preparation step for a requirement you cannot yet support.

“I cannot support this claim yet” is a useful outcome. Keep the gap visible and decide what evidence would change it. You do not have to describe every activity during a break as professional development.

## What counts as evidence here?

Evidence is something that helps you give an accurate account: a dated example you remember clearly, your own permitted work sample, a practice file, feedback you can describe honestly, or a completed exercise. It need not be public or shown to the interviewer.

An old example can demonstrate past experience without establishing current proficiency. An exercise can demonstrate practice without establishing production experience. A course completion can show learning without proving that you have used the skill at work. Record those differences rather than hiding them.

Do not recreate a confidential employer document to make your answer look stronger. You can describe the work at an appropriate level or create a clearly labeled fictional exercise using non-sensitive data.
